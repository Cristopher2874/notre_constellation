3. Problems I saw on assistance robotics (and the context I want to work on)

In Mexico and other third countries, we have access to physical therapists, and there are already centers that are in charge of helping people to recover, but in reality, the resources are not really there. Public institutions have a lot of patints (some appointments could be with six months of difference, which is not useful) and not always the required resources to help people, on the other hand, private institutions can be very expensive, and many people can't afford them.

For elements of movility is really hard for poor people to get them.

The ones that already exist are very expensive and not always adjust to real world environements or are limited by weight and real movement range. Moreover, the elements that are used for assitance depend on a profesional supervision and also on recalibrations to the sensors due to context and sourroundings problems.

I identified three main problems on assistance robotics, specificly on orthesis that are present on the market.

a. They are expensive and not accesible to population that has restricted resources.
b. They require constant adaptation and recalibration of sensors to real world environements.
c. They are heavy and limited by weight and real movement range.

I belive that with latest AI advances and the previous research that has been conducted on the area, we would be able to solve this constrains on the future.

These three problems are not separable in practice — cost, adaptability, and weight all trade off against each other, and solving only one usually makes another worse. That is exactly why this cannot be a one-person, one-discipline solution: it needs someone who understands sensing and control well enough to solve (b), but who also understands manufacturing, materials, and supply costs well enough to keep (a) and (c) honest at the same time, and who can hold a team of specialists together toward that. That is the real argument for the generalist path, not just an admissions strategy.

4. What project and career path I want to go for

In one or two sentences: my long-term goal is to build or lead an organization — my own, or one I help grow — that develops accessible, adaptive assistance devices for people with reduced mobility, starting from Mexico and built on a technical foundation and network developed in France. The near-term project I am developing during my studies is a single-joint compliant assistance module with force sensing and adaptive impedance or admittance control, which is my proof that I can build the hardest part of the chain myself before I ask anyone else to trust me leading it (othesis)

Early on, that means being technically credible enough to design and validate the sensing-and-control core myself. Later, it means knowing enough about manufacturing, materials sourcing, logistics, regulatory paths, and team leadership to run or co-run the organization that actually gets a device like this into someone's hands at a price they can afford. I do not need to be the expert in rehabilitation medicine, injection molding, or supply-chain logistics — I need to know enough about each to ask the right questions, hire or partner with the right specialists, and make good calls across the whole chain. That is the generalist bet: staying only in the integration layer forever would cap my impact at whatever a lab or a single research contract lets me touch; building toward leading the organization is what makes the bigger vision — accessible devices, not just a working prototype — actually possible.

I would like to build orthesis that are accesible for low level population, that can adapt to different environment and that are really werable day to day for the user. The project is targeted to be a future cross organization between Europre and Mexico, where the first integration is targeted to be implemented with children who need care to become functional adults.


The path I have right now is mechatronics engineering, with a layer of applied AI/software experience from Oracle, 3D printing and design thinking from LEAD 3D, and hands-on systems-building from FRC. To get from here to that 15-year goal, I need to keep building in these directions:

a. Mechanical design: materials, configurations, optimization, and manufacturing of compliant mechanisms — deep enough to design and validate a real device, not just specify one.
b. Dynamics and mechanisms: how the pieces move, forces, transmission mechanisms, actuators — including fluid-power and pneumatic or hydraulic actuation, a legitimate axis for compliant, safe-around-the-body devices.
c. Electronics and embedded systems: signals, sensors, microcontrollers, PCB design, real-time signal processing — where my Oracle background connects, as the layer that reads sensor data and executes control in real time.
d. Control theory: feedback and feedforward control, PID, system identification, and — most directly relevant to my near-term project — impedance and admittance control.
e. Applied AI and computer vision: enough to use current tools and models where they genuinely help a device adapt and recalibrate itself, without that becoming a separate research track that pulls me away from the systems work. I would like to get involved with research groups or organizations that are working on making AI and Robotics collide, using state-of-the-art models and techniques. We can see as examples current state-of-the-art protocols such as (MHS)[https://www.anthropic.com/news/model-hardware-standard-research-preview] or Google robotics model like (Gemini Robotics 2)[https://deepmind.google/models/gemini-robotics/vla/]
f. Manufacturing: 3D printing, injection molding, machining, and other processes relevant to compliant mechanisms and to eventually producing a device at meaningful scale and cost.
g. Business, logistics, and management: project and team management, enough understanding of cost structure, supply chains, and intellectual property to eventually run or co-run the organization, and enough entrepreneurship literacy to know what building that organization actually requires.

I intend to build my career centered on Mexico, which means I will need to lead teams and be comfortable working across an international supply chain and, likely, cross-border collaboration between France and Mexico.

The current degree I have at UASLP (B.S. in Mechatronics Engineering) covers a, b, c, and the basics of d and f. The double degree in France is where I get real depth in control theory and the robotics specialization, access to a lab ecosystem actively working on exactly this kind of sensing-and-control integration, and — just as important for the generalist arc — exposure to the broader engineering and management training that a "tronc commun" style généraliste program gives, which a narrower program would not.
